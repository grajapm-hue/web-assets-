🔮 One card at a time — and then the trick explained

The current Binary Number Guess shows all eight cards at once and asks the child to tap the ones containing their number. That is fast, but it gives away nothing: a child taps, a number appears, and no-one learns why. This version asks one question at a time, then animates the logic once it has guessed. Try it — it works.

The number stays in your head. Kimo never asks for it up front — if he knew it, there would be no trick. He only asks after he has guessed, and only if he got it wrong, so he can tell you which card slipped.

Secret structure

Middle = target ÷ 3. Opposite boxes always add to twice the middle — for 5 that is 10: 1+9, 2+8, 3+7, 4+6.

The count-down method

1 in the top middle. Count three down, place to the right. Blocked? Drop straight below. Repeat to 9.

What changes from todaythree fixes
1
Help reaches the board

Today the only help lives on another screen. A stuck child abandons the puzzle to find it, and often does not come back. The 💡 button keeps them in the game.

2
Graded, not all-or-nothing

Today it is “no help” or “Watch me solve the whole thing”. Nothing in between. Four levels means a child can take the smallest push that unsticks them.

3
Learn follows the puzzle you are on

The per-size logic already exists — it just is not obviously tied to the game in front of you. A switcher at the top makes that explicit, and keeps the formula sheets one tap away.

Why this is worth the extra taps

Eight yes/no questions is the whole idea of binary — one bit per question. Asking them one at a time is the lesson, not a slower version of it. The card values stay hidden until the reveal, because that is where the surprise lives — the moment 80 + 10 + 2 lands on their number.

An earlier draft asked the child to type the number first so the app could verify itself. That was wrong: if the app already knows, nothing is being read and nothing is surprising. Now the number never leaves the child's head. Kimo guesses, asks “did I get it?”, and only when the answer is no does he ask what it really was — purely so he can point at the card that was answered the wrong way round. The self-check survives; the magic survives with it.
